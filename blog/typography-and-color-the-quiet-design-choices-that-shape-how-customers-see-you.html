    Typography and Color: The Quiet Design Choices That Shape How Customers See You

    Visitors rarely say, "I trusted this company because of its typeface." Yet within moments of landing on a page, people form an impression of your business, and much of that impression comes from type and color. Crisp, readable text and a calm, consistent palette signal care and competence. Cramped lines, clashing colors, and five different fonts suggest the opposite, even when the services behind them are excellent.

    You do not need to become a designer to make better decisions here. Understanding a few principles will help you brief a design team, evaluate proposals, and keep your website consistent as it grows.

    Start With Readability, Not Personality

    It is tempting to choose fonts and colors based on mood first. A better order is to make sure everything is easy to read, then add personality within those limits. On a website, text is the product: it is how visitors learn what you do, what it costs, and how to contact you.

    A few readability fundamentals apply to almost every site:

    • Body text size: Around 16 pixels is a common baseline for body copy on screens, and larger is often better for content-heavy sites or older audiences.
    • Line height: Text set with line spacing of roughly 1.4 to 1.6 times the font size is far easier to read than tightly packed lines.
    • Line length: Lines that stretch across a wide monitor are tiring to follow. Many designers aim for roughly 50 to 75 characters per line for English text.
    • Alignment: Left-aligned text is usually easiest to scan in English. Fully justified text can create uneven gaps between words on the web.
    • Contrast: Light gray text on a white background may look elegant in a mockup, but it frustrates real readers. WCAG accessibility guidelines call for a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal-sized text.

    Choosing Typefaces That Work Together

    Most business websites need only one or two typefaces. Restraint creates consistency, and every additional font file adds weight that can slow down your pages.

    Common, reliable approaches include:

    1. One versatile family. A single typeface with several weights, such as regular, medium, and bold, can handle headings and body text gracefully.
    2. A heading font plus a body font. For example, a distinctive serif for headings paired with a clean sans-serif for paragraphs. The two should contrast clearly, not look almost-but-not-quite the same.
    3. A system font stack. Using the fonts already installed on visitors' devices loads instantly and looks native on every platform, at the cost of some brand distinctiveness.

    When evaluating a typeface, look beyond the alphabet sample. Check how it renders numbers, since prices and phone numbers matter. Confirm it includes every character you need, including any accented letters. And make sure it is properly licensed for web use. Libraries such as Google Fonts offer many open-licensed options.

    Building a Clear Type Hierarchy

    Hierarchy tells readers what matters most and how content is organized. Without it, every line competes for attention and nothing stands out.

    Good hierarchy uses a small, consistent set of styles:

    • A main page heading that is clearly the largest element
    • Two or three levels of subheadings, each visibly distinct
    • Body text, plus a slightly smaller style for captions and notes
    • A consistent style for links and buttons so people instantly recognize what is clickable

    Size is only one tool. Weight, spacing, and color also create hierarchy. Generous space above a heading, for example, groups it with the content below and separates it from what came before. Document these choices in a simple style guide so every new page follows the same rules.

    Building a Color Palette With Purpose

    Color carries meaning and guides attention. A website palette typically has three kinds of colors:

    • Neutrals: Backgrounds, body text, borders, and dividers. These make up most of the page.
    • Brand colors: One or two colors that express your identity, used for headers, highlights, and key graphics.
    • Accent or action color: A color reserved for primary buttons and important links, so calls to action stand out consistently.

    Many designers use the 60-30-10 guideline as a starting point: roughly 60 percent dominant neutral color, 30 percent secondary color, and 10 percent accent. It is a rule of thumb, not a law, but it helps prevent pages that feel either flat or chaotic.

    Also define colors for feedback states, such as success, warning, and error, and never rely on color alone to communicate them. Pair a red border with a clear text message, for example, so the meaning reaches people with color vision deficiencies too.

    Keep in mind that color associations vary by culture and industry. Test your palette with people from your actual audience, especially if you serve customers across different countries.

    Special Considerations for Japanese Typography

    If your site includes Japanese text, typography requires extra thought:

    • Font file size: Japanese fonts contain thousands of characters, so files are much larger than Latin fonts. Techniques such as subsetting, or using services that deliver only the characters a page needs, keep pages fast.
    • Line height: Japanese text generally benefits from more generous line spacing than English.
    • Line breaks: Without spaces between words, line breaks can fall awkwardly. Modern CSS offers settings to improve how lines wrap.
    • Pairing scripts: When Japanese and English appear side by side, choose fonts whose Latin characters and Japanese characters feel balanced. Families like Noto Sans JP were designed with this in mind.

    Thoughtful typography and color will not make a website on their own, but they quietly support everything else on the page. Once your choices are set, apply them everywhere, from email signatures to printed brochures, so customers recognize you instantly.
