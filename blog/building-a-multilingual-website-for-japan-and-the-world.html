    Building a Multilingual Website for Japan and the World

    For a business based in Japan, a second language on your website can open doors: international tourists planning a visit to Osaka, overseas companies looking for a reliable partner, or foreign residents who would rather read in English. For businesses abroad, a Japanese version can be the difference between being considered and being ignored by Japanese customers, who often expect content in their own language and in a style that feels familiar.

    But a multilingual website is much more than a translated copy of your current pages. Done well, it feels native in every language. Done poorly, it can confuse visitors and search engines alike, and quietly undermine the trust you worked hard to build. This guide covers the decisions that matter most.

    Translation Is Not the Same as Localization

    Translation converts words from one language into another. Localization adapts the whole experience for a particular audience, including words, examples, formats, imagery, and even which information comes first.

    Consider a few differences between Japanese and English-speaking audiences that affect a website:

    • Tone and formality. Japanese business writing typically uses polite forms and a more formal register. A casual, punchy English headline translated directly can feel out of place.
    • Information density. Japanese websites often present more detail up front, including specifications, pricing tables, and access information. Many Western sites favor shorter text and more white space. Neither is wrong; each matches different expectations.
    • Formats. Dates, addresses, names, and phone numbers follow different conventions. Japanese addresses usually run from the largest unit (prefecture) to the smallest, with a postal code first, while English addresses typically run the other way.
    • Trust signals. Company profile details, such as founding date, registered address, and representative's name, are commonly expected on Japanese business sites.
    • Contact and payment preferences. Messaging apps such as LINE are widely used in Japan, and some customers expect payment options like bank transfer or convenience store payment.

    Machine translation tools have improved and can help produce first drafts. For anything customer-facing, however, have a fluent human, ideally a native speaker who knows your industry, review and adapt the result.

    Choosing a URL Structure

    Each language version should have its own URL so search engines can index it and visitors can share it. There are three common approaches:

    Structure Example Considerations
    Subdirectory example.com/en/ Simple to manage; all languages share one domain's authority
    Subdomain en.example.com Can be hosted separately; slightly more setup
    Country domain example.jp, example.com Strong local signal; more domains to buy and maintain

    For most small and mid-size businesses, subdirectories are the most practical choice. Whatever you choose, avoid showing different languages at the same URL based on cookies or browser settings alone, because search engines may only ever see one version.

    The Technical Details That Search Engines Need

    A few behind-the-scenes elements help search engines show the right version to the right person:

    1. The lang attribute. Each page's HTML should declare its language, for example lang="ja" or lang="en". This also helps screen readers pronounce content correctly.
    2. Hreflang tags. These tell search engines that pages are equivalents in different languages, so a Japanese searcher sees the Japanese page and an English searcher sees the English one. Each version should list all versions, including itself.
    3. An x-default. This hreflang value names a fallback page for visitors whose language you do not specifically serve.
    4. Translated metadata. Page titles, meta descriptions, image alt text, and URL slugs should be localized, not left in the original language.
    5. Separate keyword research. People do not search the way a dictionary translates. Research the actual terms used in each language, including whether Japanese searchers use kanji, katakana, or English loanwords for your services.

    Google is the dominant search engine in Japan, and Yahoo! JAPAN's search results are powered by Google's technology, so these standard practices serve you well in both.

    Designing for More Than One Language

    Multilingual sites need flexible design, because languages behave differently on the page.

    • Text length varies. The same message can be noticeably longer or shorter in different languages. Buttons, menus, and headings must accommodate this without breaking.
    • Fonts must support every script. Japanese fonts are large files with thousands of characters, so plan font loading carefully to keep pages fast.
    • Avoid text inside images. Text baked into graphics cannot be translated easily, read by search engines, or resized.
    • Respect line spacing. Japanese text usually needs more generous line height than English to remain comfortable to read.

    Making Language Switching Easy

    Your language switcher should be easy to find and easy to understand:

    • Place it in a consistent spot, commonly the top right of the header.
    • Label languages in their own language: "日本語" and "English," not "Japanese" and "English" for everyone.
    • Avoid using flags alone. Flags represent countries, not languages.
    • Link each page to its equivalent in the other language rather than dumping visitors on the homepage.
    • Suggest a language based on browser settings if you like, but never force a redirect. Let people choose.

    Keeping Every Language Current

    The hardest part of a multilingual site is often maintenance. A new service added only in Japanese, or a price change that never reaches the English pages, quickly erodes trust. Choose a CMS or workflow with proper multilingual support, assign responsibility for each language, and include translation time in every content update. If you cannot maintain every page in every language, it is better to offer a smaller, accurate set of translated pages than a large, outdated one. A shared glossary of key terms, such as service names, product names, and preferred phrasing, also helps different writers and translators stay consistent, and makes future updates faster and less expensive.
